## 2.2 The Pickup and Delivery Problem with Time Windows
    The Pickup and Delivery Problem with Time Windows (PDPTW) generalises the classical vehicle routing problem by requiring that each transportation request be associated with a distinct pickup and a distinct delivery location, rather than a single service point. A fleet of capacitated vehicles must be routed to satisfy a set of such requests at minimum cost, subject to three interlocking constraints: pairing, requiring that the pickup and delivery of any given request be served by the same vehicle; precedence, requiring that the pickup be visited before its delivery; and time windows, restricting the interval during which service may begin at each node (Dumas, Desrosiers and Soumis, 1991). Savelsbergh and Sol (1995) frame this as a constrained special case of the more general pickup and delivery problem, in which loads move directly between origin–destination pairs without transshipment.

    The combinatorial structure that makes PDPTW useful for modelling real logistics is also what makes it computationally severe. Lenstra and Rinnooy Kan (1981) established that essentially every interesting variant of the vehicle routing problem is NP-hard, even in the absence of time-window constraints. PDPTW inherits this hardness and compounds it: the pairing and precedence requirements reduce the feasible route space, while time windows further compress the feasibility landscape by making many otherwise legal insertions inadmissible. Consequently, the size of instance that can be solved exactly grows only slowly with algorithmic advances, and heuristic approaches dominate any deployment at operational scale.

    Exact methods for PDPTW have converged on branch-and-price and, more recently, branch-and-cut-and-price. Dumas et al. (1991) introduced the first PDPTW-specific exact algorithm, formulating the problem as set partitioning and pricing feasible routes via a constrained shortest path solved by dynamic programming; the largest instance solved in that paper contained 55 requests. Two decades later, Ropke and Cordeau (2009) combined column generation with dynamically added valid inequalities in a branch-and-cut-and-price scheme, solving to optimality several Li–Lim benchmark instances with up to 500 requests when time windows were tight — the largest PDPTW instances solved exactly in the literature at the time. These benchmark instances descend directly from the VRPTW test set of Solomon (1987), which remains the de facto standard for computational comparison in the field.

    Beyond this exact frontier, practical instance sizes and looser time-window structures remain out of reach, and the applied PDPTW literature is dominated by construction heuristics, metaheuristics, and — increasingly — hybrid learning-based methods. Two surveys map this space authoritatively: Berbeglia, Cordeau, Gribkovskaia and Laporte (2007) provide a three-field classification scheme covering the full family of pickup-and-delivery problems, while Parragh, Doerner and Hartl (2008) give a companion taxonomy of mathematical formulations and exact, heuristic, and metaheuristic solution methods for the vehicle-routing subfamily to which PDPTW belongs.

    PDPTW has since spawned a substantial family of structural variants — one-to-one and one-to-many, static and dynamic, with backhauls, transfers, LIFO loading, and edge-level scheduling. The variant this dissertation targets, PDPTW-SE, belongs to a specific and relatively recent subfamily of edge-scheduled formulations examined in §2.3.

## 2.6 Large language models for algorithm discovery
     The founding work of this paradigm, FunSearch (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024), showed that a pretrained code LLM paired with a systematic evaluator can advance the state of the art on established open problems, in both pure mathematics and combinatorial optimisation. Its conceptual pivot is to evolve short programs that generate solutions rather than the solutions themselves, since structured problems admit far more compact program-level descriptions than raw enumerations. Four ingredients drive the loop: a frozen LLM as proposer, an evaluator that discards incorrect outputs, an island-based population that preserves diversity across sub-groups, and best-shot prompting, in which the highest-scoring programs are fed back into the LLM's context as few-shot examples. Applied to the cap set problem, FunSearch improved the asymptotic lower bound on cap set capacity from 2.2180 to 2.2202 — the largest improvement in two decades — while a parallel bin-packing demonstration produced heuristics that beat first-fit and best-fit and generalised to instances larger than those seen in training. Because outputs are readable code rather than opaque numerical objects, researchers can inspect discovered programs for structural insight, extracting verifiable knowledge from the search.
     The second landmark in this line of work, Evolution of Heuristics (EoH; Liu et al., 2024), argues that searching directly in the space of code — as FunSearch does — leaves the LLM's most powerful capability unused: reasoning in natural language. EoH therefore co-evolves two coupled representations of each heuristic — a natural-language thought describing the underlying idea and its executable code — using the LLM as an evolutionary operator guided by five explicit prompt strategies split between exploration (E1, E2) and modification (M1–M3). The payoff is sample efficiency: on online bin packing, EoH matches or beats FunSearch's heuristics using roughly two thousand LLM queries against FunSearch's approximately one million, while generalising more robustly to out-of-distribution instances. Similar gains hold on the Travelling Salesman and Flow Shop Scheduling problems against both classical and neural baselines. Ablations confirm that the thought layer contributes real signal, without which the code-only variant collapses.
     The third landmark, ReEvo (Ye et al., 2024), adds a critique step to the loop: a separate "reflector" LLM compares each pair of parent heuristics in natural language, and these short-term reflections are distilled into longer-term design hints — what the authors term verbal gradients within the heuristic space. Where FunSearch and EoH used the LLM only as a generator, ReEvo mirrors how human experts iterate — proposing, critiquing, revising. Across six combinatorial optimisation problems and five algorithmic families, it matches or beats EoH with fewer evaluations.
     Across these three landmarks the recipe is stable: a systematic evaluator drives fitness-based evolution over LLM-generated code, differing only in what the LLM is asked to do inside the loop. Applications so far span pure mathematics (FunSearch), bin packing, flow shop scheduling, and the classical routing benchmarks — TSP, CVRP, OP — but no published work applies the paradigm to PDPTW-SE, a variant introduced only in January 2026 by Barbosa, Tiwari and Melo. §2.7 develops this positioning.
